A beginner's guide to QuantDesk

Four models read the same stock. Here is how to read them back.

QuantDesk shows you a company through four independent lenses — who is trading it, what its price has done, what the business is worth, and whether its accounts can be trusted. This manual explains every one of those lenses, and every term on every screen, assuming you have never opened a financial statement in your life.

00 — Start here

What this tool is, and what it refuses to be

Most stock research tools give you one opinion. QuantDesk gives you four, computed from deliberately different data, and shows you where they agree and where they don't. The bet behind the whole design is simple: any single method can be fooled. A company can look cheap on a spreadsheet while quietly running out of cash. A chart can look strong because one large fund happened to rebalance that week. When methods that share no inputs land in the same place, that agreement is worth more than any one of them shouting.

The two families, and why colour means something here

The four lenses do not split evenly. Two of them — Flow and Trend — are computed from the same daily record of prices and trading volume. They are not two independent opinions; they are two questions asked of one dataset. The other two — Value and Quality — read the company's financial statements, and carry most of the genuinely separate information.

That split is why this manual is coloured the way it is, and why the app is too. Cool hues are the price family. Warm hues are the filings family. A colour in this document never decorates — it tells you which body of data is speaking.

Read this before anything else

This is a research and teaching tool. It is not investment advice, and nothing in it is a recommendation to buy or sell. Every number it shows is an estimate built on assumptions you can change, on data scraped from a free source that is sometimes wrong or missing. A tool that says “undervalued” is telling you what a model concluded from the inputs it was given — not what will happen. Losing money in markets is normal and easy; the purpose of learning this material is to understand what you don't know, not to acquire confidence.

The one habit worth taking from this app

QuantDesk was built around an unusual discipline: every number has to say how much it can be trusted. Where an estimate is too noisy to report, the app reports a bound instead of a figure. Where a model doesn't apply to a company, it refuses rather than printing a number anyway. Where a signal has been tested and found not to work, it says so on screen.

If you take one thing from this manual into the rest of your life as an analyst, take that. The skill that separates a quant from someone with a spreadsheet is not knowing more formulas. It is knowing which of your numbers are load-bearing and which are decoration.

That discipline is also why the app answers “so what?” in sentences rather than with a rating. The panel that does it is covered in section 03, and it is the fastest route into everything else here.

01 — Foundations

Words you need before any of the numbers make sense

Skip this if you already know what a drawdown is. If you don't, everything downstream will be fog until you do. These fifteen ideas are the entire vocabulary the rest of the manual assumes.

The instrument

  • Share (or stock, or equity). A unit of ownership in a company. Own one share of a company that has issued a hundred, and you own one percent of it.
  • Ticker. The short code a stock trades under. AAPL is Apple. Indonesian listings carry a .JK suffix — BBCA.JK is Bank Central Asia. The suffix is not cosmetic: BBCA without it is a completely different security listed in the US, and confusing the two is a real failure mode this app is explicitly built to prevent.
  • Shares outstanding. How many shares exist. Needed to turn “the whole business is worth X” into “one share is worth X divided by that.”
  • Market capitalisation (market cap). Share price × shares outstanding. What the market currently says the whole company is worth.

The data

  • OHLCV. The five numbers recorded for every trading day: the Open, the High, the Low, the Close, and the Volume (how many shares changed hands). Nearly every price-based calculation in this app is built from these five columns and nothing else.
  • Bar (or candle). One day's OHLCV, drawn as one mark on a chart.
  • Trading day. Markets are shut at weekends and on holidays, so a year is roughly 252 trading days, not 365. When you see 252 in a formula, that is what it means.

Return and risk

  • Return. The percentage change in price over some period. Up from 100 to 110 is a 10% return.
  • Compounding. Returns stack multiplicatively, not additively. Up 50% then down 50% leaves you at 75, not 100. This is why a big loss hurts more than an equal-sized gain helps, and it is the single most important piece of arithmetic in investing.
  • CAGR. Compound Annual Growth Rate — the one steady yearly rate that would have produced the actual start-to-finish result. It smooths away everything that happened in between, which is both its use and its danger.
  • Volatility. How much the price bounces around, usually expressed as an annualised percentage. High volatility means a wilder ride in both directions.
  • Drawdown. How far the price has fallen from its own previous peak. The maximum drawdown is the worst such fall in the history you're looking at — the number that tells you what holding this actually felt like.
  • Benchmark. The thing you compare against. For a US stock it's usually the S&P 500 (^GSPC); for an Indonesian one, the Jakarta Composite (^JKSE). This matters because your real alternative was never cash — it was buying the whole market instead.
  • Liquidity. How easily you can buy or sell without moving the price against yourself. A thinly traded stock is illiquid: your own order pushes the price.
  • Bid–ask spread. At any instant there is a highest price a buyer will pay (the bid) and a lowest a seller will accept (the ask). The gap between them is a cost you pay on every round trip, before the price has moved at all.
The idea that makes the rest click

A percentile is a position within a group, not a score. If a stock is at the 90th percentile for momentum in a scan of the Nasdaq-100, that means it beat 90% of the other names in that scan, on that day. It does not mean it scored 90 out of 100 on some absolute scale. QuantDesk converts nearly everything in its ranking tier to percentiles for exactly this reason: a position within a stated group is a claim the data supports, and a score out of 100 usually isn't.

This matters more than it sounds, and it is the fix for the hardest problem a beginner has with this app: every other figure on screen is absolute, and an absolute number is meaningless without priors you do not yet have. Section 05 covers the panel that turns them into positions.

02 — Walkthrough

Your first session, step by step

  1. Type a ticker and press Run all lenses. Start with something large and familiar — AAPL, MSFT, BBCA.JK. Large companies have complete data, so you see the app working as designed before you meet its gaps.
  2. Leave the mode on Guided. Guided is not a lesser mode. It shows each headline number with its interpretation already written out, folds the expert tuning controls behind one labelled disclosure, and drops the raw indicator grid. Full is every control and every indicator, unchanged. Nothing is computed differently between them — only what is put in front of you.
  3. Read the top strip — the confluence rail. Four panels, one per lens, each with the question it answers written above its verdict. Above them sits the agreement line: something like “1 of 2 independent readings negative · 4 lenses, 2 independent sources.” Note that it counts sources, not panels, for the reason explained in section 00.
  4. Then read “What this adds up to” underneath it. This is the answer to so what?, written as sentences, and it is where a beginner should spend their first five minutes. Section 03 is entirely about how to read it.
  5. Click any i icon. Every number in the app has one. It opens three sentences: what the metric measures, what this particular value means, and what would make you act differently — or an explicit admission that nothing would, because most numbers are context rather than triggers.
  6. Now open the tabs, in this order: Value and Quality first, then Trend, then Flow. That is roughly the order of how much the evidence supports them — which is the opposite of the order most beginners read in.
  7. On the Trend tab, press Compare with peers. It takes a few seconds and it is the fastest way to learn whether the numbers you are reading are ordinary. Do this on the first few companies you look at, even if you never do it again — it is how you acquire the priors the rest of the app assumes you have.
  8. When something is missing, believe the gap. If Quality says “not applicable” for a bank, that is a designed refusal, not a bug. If Value offers you a manual-input form, the data source genuinely had a hole in it.
A word on evidence grades

Throughout the app, readings carry a grade: strong moderate weak none. This is not how confident the app is in the arithmetic — the arithmetic is always right. It is how well the underlying effect is supported by published, replicated, out-of-sample research. A twelve-month momentum reading and a candlestick pattern are not the same class of claim, and showing them in identical type would be the lie. Weight what you read accordingly.

03 — The answer to “so what?”

What it adds up to, and why there is no score

Four verdicts side by side is progress, but it is not an answer. The panel below the confluence rail — What this adds up to — is the app saying, in plain sentences, what the four lenses collectively found. It is the first thing a beginner should read and the part this manual would point you to if you only had five minutes.

The five blocks, in the order they appear

The order is the argument, and it is deliberate. The evidence comes before the summary, and what the app cannot tell you comes before what to do next.

Why there is no buy / hold / sell

The obvious version of this feature is a single verdict, or a conviction score out of 100. It is refused, permanently, and the reasoning is worth understanding because it applies far beyond this app.

Everything in QuantDesk works to establish how much its own numbers can bear: that the four lenses rest on two datasets, that seven ranking columns carry about 3.4 signals' worth of information, that a discounted cash flow is typically 60–80% perpetuity guess, that several readings are graded weak. A composite score discards every one of those findings — and it discards them in the one field everybody would read. The moment it exists, nobody opens the four lenses again.

The general lesson

Averaging things you have just finished proving are correlated, and presenting the result as a consensus, is the easiest way to build a model that looks robust and is not. If you take one habit from this section: when you are tempted to collapse several measurements into one number, ask what the number destroys. Usually it is the disagreement, and usually the disagreement was the finding.

A worked example

Run on Apple, the panel reported four readings — Flow quiet, Trend constructive on 6 of 8 long-horizon checks, Value overvalued, Quality neutral — and then said this:

What it actually printed
Do they agree?
“They disagree: price and volume read constructively while the filings do not. The disagreement is the finding. It usually means the market is pricing something the accounts have not shown yet, or that the accounts are showing something the price has not reacted to — and which of those it is cannot be settled from this page.”
The tension, named
“Rising past what the business supports. The price trend is constructive and the model says the price has run beyond what the cash flows justify. Both can be true, and both can persist for years — momentum and valuation operate on completely different clocks.”
What it could not tell you
That the Hurst exponent cannot separate this price series from a random walk, so every price-based reading on the page should be downgraded — and that 71% of the fair value came from the terminal assumption rather than the forecast years.

Now notice what a score would have done to that. Three constructive-ish readings and one negative averages to something mildly positive, and the two sentences that actually matter — the trend tools may be measuring noise, and most of the valuation is one assumption — disappear entirely. The number would have been more confident and less true.

How to read a disagreement

Disagreement between the price family and the filings family is the most common result, and it is not a failure of the tool. It means one of two things, and the app is explicit that it cannot tell you which:

  • The market knows something the accounts have not shown yet. Filings are backward-looking and quarterly; prices move on expectations. A stock can look expensive on last year's cash flows precisely because next year's will be larger.
  • The accounts are showing something the price has not reacted to. The opposite case, and the one where the filings-based lenses earn their weight.

Settling it requires information from outside this app — the business, the industry, the filings themselves. What the synthesis does is make sure you know there is a question to settle, rather than reading three green panels and stopping.

When a lens refuses, the count changes

On a bank, the Quality lens returns an explicit refusal rather than a score, and the synthesis then reports three lenses reporting rather than four — and states plainly that half the filings-side evidence is missing, so the valuation is carrying that side alone. Watch for that line. A cross-check with one leg missing is a much weaker claim than one with two, and it would be easy to miss if the panel simply showed a gap.

04 — Lens one · price family

Flow: is anyone unusual trading this?

Every day, a stock produces a price move and a volume figure. Most days are unremarkable. Occasionally a day looks nothing like the others — a big move on enormous volume, or heavy volume with almost no move at all. The Flow lens hunts for those days and asks whether they look like buying or selling.

How it finds them: the Isolation Forest

An Isolation Forest is a machine-learning method for finding oddities without being told in advance what odd looks like. The intuition is genuinely simple. Imagine repeatedly splitting your data with random cuts. A point sitting in the middle of the crowd takes many cuts to isolate from its neighbours. A point way out on its own takes very few. The algorithm builds hundreds of random splitting trees, measures how quickly each day gets isolated, and the easily-isolated days are the anomalies.

It is unsupervised — nobody labelled which days were “real” institutional activity. That is a strength (no human bias about what to look for) and a weakness (no guarantee what it finds means anything). QuantDesk feeds it six numbers per day: the return, volume relative to average, the size of the move ignoring direction, the Money Flow Index, an on-balance-volume z-score, and the intraday range.

Jargon in that paragraph, unpacked
Relative volume (RVOL)
Today's volume divided by the recent average. 3.0 means three times a normal day's trading.
On-balance volume (OBV)
A running total that adds the day's volume when the price closed up and subtracts it when the price closed down. It asks whether volume has been arriving on up days or down days.
Z-score
How many standard deviations a value sits from its own average. A z-score of +2 means “unusually high, in the top few percent.” It is the universal way to make different quantities comparable.
Money Flow Index (MFI)
Like a speedometer of buying vs selling pressure from 0 to 100, but weighted by how much money changed hands rather than price alone.

The second detector: CUSUM, and the problem it solves

There is something the first model structurally cannot see. A large institution building a position deliberately splits its order across weeks precisely so that no single day stands out. A detector that scores each day on its own merits can only ever catch the impatient buyer.

CUSUM — cumulative sum — solves this by accumulating small deviations instead of judging them individually. Each day's small excess is added to a running total; when the total crosses a threshold, an episode is flagged. A long run of unremarkable days can trip it even though no individual day would. On Apple this surfaced a 27-day accumulation episode at just 1.04× average volume — completely invisible to any volume-spike rule.

Trading costs, and why the panel sometimes says "at most"

A move of 0.3% sounds like information until you learn it costs 0.4% to get in and out. The Flow panel therefore estimates the bid–ask spread from daily bars using two published estimators (Corwin–Schultz and Abdi–Ranaldo), and compares the day's move against it.

Here is the part worth internalising, because it is a general lesson about estimation. Both estimators have a noise floor — a level of resolution below which they cannot distinguish a real spread from zero. That floor was measured by simulation, with the true spread set to zero, and comes out proportional to the stock's volatility. On a mega-cap with a genuinely tiny spread, the floor sits an order of magnitude above the real number, so the estimator would happily report a cost the stock does not charge. Below the floor the app now reports a ceiling — “at most X” — instead of a measurement.

The transferable idea

Every estimator has a resolution limit, and it is your job to know it. An instrument that cannot measure below a certain size does not return zero when the true value is small — it returns noise, and noise reported as a measurement is worse than no answer.

What this lens cannot tell you

Whether the trader was an institution at all. Index rebalances, options expiry, dividend dates and earnings announcements all produce identical footprints. “Unusual” means statistically unusual, nothing more. On a thinly traded stock, “heavy volume moved the price” often just means the order book was shallow.

The app is unusually blunt about this: it ships an event study (section 09) whose entire job is to measure whether these flags predict anything at all, and on some tickers the honest answer is no.

Trend: what the price did, across three horizons

This is the largest lens, and it is organised by holding period, longest first. That ordering is deliberate: reading it left to right walks you from the strongest evidence to the weakest.

Long horizon — the section that matters most

A checklist runs first: is price above its 200-day average, does Faber's 10-month rule say invested or out, what is 12-1 momentum doing, how strong is the trend, is the series even trending at all. Then come the three ideas worth understanding properly.

Rolling returns — the fix for a lying CAGR
The problem
A headline CAGR describes exactly one start date and one end date. Change the start by six months and the number can double or vanish. Quoting it as “the return” is describing one lucky draw as if it were the population.
What the app does
It computes every overlapping holding period in the history — every possible 1-year window, every 3-year window, every 5-year — and reports the distribution. So instead of one CAGR you get “the worst 3-year window in this history returned −4%, and 87% of them were positive.”
Why you should care
It answers the question you actually have — “what if I buy at a bad moment?” — rather than the question a single CAGR answers, which is “what if I had bought on precisely this date years ago?”
The Ulcer index — measuring what holding actually feels like
The problem
Maximum drawdown records depth and forgets duration. A 30% fall that recovers in two months and a 30% fall that grinds sideways for three years produce the same number, and are nothing like the same experience.
What it is
The Ulcer index (Martin & McCann, 1989) squares the depth below the previous peak at every point and averages it over time — so both how far and how long enter the score. A long shallow grind scores worse than a sharp crash that recovers.
Why you should care
Most people don't sell at the bottom because the loss was large. They sell because it went on for so long that they stopped believing. This is the metric that models that.
The Hurst exponent — the app's own honesty check
What it measures
Whether a price series has memory. H = 0.5 is a pure random walk: past moves tell you nothing about the next one. Above 0.5 suggests persistence (trends continue); below suggests mean reversion (moves snap back).
Why it is the honesty check
If H sits near 0.5, then every trend indicator on the page is describing noise. It is the one reading whose job is to tell you the other readings may be meaningless.
The part worth studying
The estimator is noisy. Tested against artificially generated series with a known true H, its standard error is about 0.05 on five years of daily bars. The app originally judged it against a fixed 0.45–0.55 band — barely one standard error wide — which labelled a genuine random walk as trending 35% of the time. The band is now sized from how much data you actually have, and a short history returns “cannot tell” rather than a guess.

Short and mid horizons — and an unusual warning

These sections answer a different question: not “could I have held this?” but “if I were buying in the next few weeks, where are the levels?” They produce support and resistance, a stop-loss, a target, and a position size.

The app argues against its own feature here

Over one to four weeks, prices have historically shown mild reversal rather than continuation — the opposite of the twelve-month momentum effect. The panel states this rather than hiding it. Short-horizon technical analysis is where the published evidence is weakest and where costs bite hardest, and the app grades it accordingly.

The short-horizon vocabulary

  • Support and resistance. Price levels where the market has previously turned around — a floor buyers defended, a ceiling sellers defended. QuantDesk reports how many times price actually turned at each level, because a level touched once is a coincidence and a level touched five times is a fact about the order book.
  • Stop-loss. A pre-decided exit price at which you accept the trade idea was wrong. Placed just beyond structure, with a buffer of one or more ATR.
  • ATR (Average True Range). How much this stock typically moves in a day, including overnight gaps. It is the natural unit for stop placement: a stop 1 ATR away will be hit by ordinary noise, which is why the app refuses to place one tighter than that and says why.
  • Risk/reward ratio. Distance to your target divided by distance to your stop. 2.0 means you stand to make twice what you'd lose. It says nothing about probability — a 5:1 setup that works one time in ten is a losing strategy.
  • Position size. How much of your account to commit so that being stopped out costs exactly your chosen risk budget and no more. This is the single most useful idea in the section, and the least glamorous.
  • Pivot points. Levels arithmetically derived from the previous complete period's high, low and close. Widely watched, which is arguably why they sometimes work.
  • VWAP (volume-weighted average price). The average price actually paid by everyone who traded over a window. Above it, the average buyer since the anchor date is in profit; below it, under water.
  • Donchian breakout. Price closing above the highest high of the last N days. The classic trend-following entry rule.
  • Bollinger squeeze. A period when volatility bands narrow unusually — the price has gone quiet. Often precedes a large move, though it does not tell you which way.
  • Divergence. Price making a new high that the momentum reading does not confirm. Read as a move losing steam. Evidence for it is weak.
  • Gap. A price band the stock jumped straight over between one close and the next open. Almost nobody traded inside it, so there is no established support in there.

Compare with peers — the antidote to a number with no frame

Everything above this line is absolute. The worst fall was 33%. Volatility is 28%. The return was 16% a year. If you have been reading markets for a decade those numbers place themselves; if you have not, they are unreadable — not because they are complicated, but because nothing tells you whether they are ordinary or alarming. This is the single biggest obstacle between a beginner and this app, and no amount of extra explanation fixes it. What fixes it is a denominator.

The Compare with peers button on this tab places the company against the other members of its own index and says the result in sentences. On Apple it reports:

The same numbers, with a frame around them
Worst fall
33% — and milder than 95% of the Nasdaq-100. The figure that looked frightening on its own turns out to be one of the gentlest in its index.
Day-to-day swing
25% — calmer than 90% of the Nasdaq-100.
Momentum
+43% over the year — but that is only the 72nd percentile. A number that sounds spectacular in isolation is unremarkable among its peers, which is exactly the correction that only a comparison can make.

Notice that the correction runs in both directions. A scary number can turn out to be ordinary, and an impressive one can turn out to be middling. Neither is available from an absolute figure, and both change what you think.

Four things to hold on to when you read it

  • A percentile is a position, not a rating. The 95th percentile does not mean “95 out of 100”. It means that in this group, on this date, 95% of the members scored worse on this measure. The bar on the panel has a mark at the midpoint for exactly this reason: it is a distribution, not a scoreboard.
  • Change the group and the company moves. Apple sits differently against the Nasdaq-100 than against the Dow's thirty. Neither is the truth; they are two different questions. Where a company belongs to more than one index the panel offers the switch, and it defaults to the larger — a percentile against twenty-nine peers can only place a stock in thirds, which is barely a comparison.
  • A high position on a weak signal is not good news. Two of the seven measures are graded weak, and the panel refuses to colour them green however well the company places. Being in the top decile on something that does not predict anything is still not predicting anything. That restraint is deliberate and it is worth internalising: rank on a bad measure is still a bad measure.
  • Only price is compared. All seven measures come from price and volume. There is no peer comparison for the valuation or the accounts, because company filings cannot be downloaded in a single batch the way price history can. The panel states this rather than letting you assume the whole company was ranked.
The trap this panel is most likely to set

A row of high percentiles looks like a verdict, and it is not one. It says this company has recently behaved better than most of its index on measures computed from its price. That is a description of the past, in a group you chose, on a day you happened to look. The panel also reports how much the seven measures overlap — typically about three signals' worth of independent information rather than seven — so a strong combined placing is a weaker claim than the seven bars suggest.

Two design decisions worth copying

Setups are pre-registered. The app checks a fixed list of configurations in a fixed order, and “none of them is present” is the most common answer. A test fires the detector across twenty random walks and fails the build if a majority produce a trade. This is how you stop a pattern detector from finding patterns in noise.

Candlestick patterns are firewalled. They are detected, graded weak, and forbidden from placing an entry, stop or target. Multi-bar chart patterns — head and shoulders, flags, wedges, cup-and-handle — are named on the panel as things the app will not claim to detect, with the reason for each. Declining to guess is a feature.

06 — Lens three · filings family

Value: what is the business actually worth?

Everything so far looked at the price. This lens ignores the price entirely until the last step, works out what the company is worth from its own cash flows, and only then compares that to what the market is charging.

The one idea underneath all of it: discounting

A dollar next year is worth less than a dollar today — you could have invested today's dollar, and next year's might not arrive. So to value a company you forecast the cash it will produce and then shrink each future year's cash by a rate reflecting how long you wait and how risky the wait is. That rate is the discount rate. Add up all the shrunk future cash and you have the present value of the business. That is the whole of valuation theory in three sentences; everything else is argument about the inputs.

Terms you will meet on this panel

  • Free cash flow (FCF). Cash from operations minus the money spent maintaining and growing the asset base. Roughly, what's genuinely left over.
  • Beta. How much this stock moves when the whole market moves. Beta 1.5 means it historically moved 1.5× as hard as the index, in both directions. It feeds the discount rate: more market-sensitive, higher required return.
  • Equity risk premium (ERP). The extra annual return investors demand for holding shares instead of government bonds. QuantDesk uses 5.5% for the US and 7.0% for Indonesia.
  • Risk-free rate. What you'd earn with (near) certainty — the government bond yield. The floor every other required return is built on.
  • WACC. Weighted Average Cost of Capital. A company funded by both equity and debt has a blended cost of money; the DCF discounts at this.
  • Terminal value. The model forecasts five years explicitly and then assumes the business continues forever at a fixed modest growth rate. The value of that “forever” is the terminal value.
  • Book value. Assets minus liabilities as recorded in the accounts — the company's net worth on paper.
  • Payout ratio. The share of earnings paid out as dividends.
The number that should worry you

The panel reports what share of the answer is terminal value, and on a typical DCF that share is 60–80%. Read that carefully: most of the valuation is not coming from the five years you forecast. It is coming from a guess about a perpetuity. A DCF is an opinion with arithmetic attached, and this is the number that tells you how much opinion is in there.

Monte Carlo: why the output is a range, not a number

Change the growth assumption by two percentage points and a DCF answer can move by half. Reporting a single “fair value” from assumptions that uncertain is false precision. So instead of running the model once, QuantDesk runs it 10,000 times, each run drawing slightly different values for growth, discount rate and terminal growth from plausible distributions. The result is a spread of outcomes.

  • Monte Carlo simulation. Any method that answers a question by running it thousands of times with randomly varied inputs and looking at the distribution of answers. Named after the casino.
  • P5 / P50 / P95. Percentiles of that distribution. P50 is the median — half the runs came out above, half below. P5 and P95 bracket the middle 90%. A wide P5–P95 gap is the model telling you it isn't confident.
  • Probability undervalued. The fraction of the 10,000 runs in which the business came out worth more than its market price. This is not the probability the stock goes up — it is the probability that this model, with these assumptions, calls it cheap.

The dispersion used in those draws is not an invented constant. An earlier version used a flat 2% standard deviation that, as the project's own notes put it, came from nowhere and set the entire width of the fan chart. It now comes from the company's own historical variability.

What this lens cannot tell you

Anything with confidence. That is not modesty, it is arithmetic — which is exactly why every assumption is an editable field. The most valuable way to use this panel is backwards: instead of asking “what does the model say it's worth?”, change the growth rate until the model's answer equals today's price, and read off what growth rate the market is already assuming. Then ask yourself whether you believe it. That converts an opinion into a testable claim, and it is how professionals actually use a DCF.

07 — Lens four · filings family

Quality: is the business sound, and are the numbers real?

A valuation is only as good as the accounts it was built from, and accounts are prepared by people with incentives. This lens runs three published academic screens over the same filings the Value lens used.

Piotroski F-Score — nine yes/no health checks

Joseph Piotroski (2000) asked whether simple accounting signals could separate improving companies from deteriorating ones. His score is nine binary tests; you score one point per pass, so the range is 0–9. Higher is better. These are the nine, as QuantDesk computes them:

#CheckIn plain English
1Return on assets positiveIs it profitable at all, relative to its size?
2Operating cash flow positiveIs real cash coming in, not just accounting profit?
3Return on assets improvingIs profitability better than last year?
4Cash flow exceeds accounting profitIs reported profit backed by cash? If not, the profit is a promise.
5Leverage fallingIs it taking on less debt?
6Current ratio improvingCan it cover short-term bills more easily than before?
7No dilutionDid it avoid issuing new shares? New shares shrink your slice.
8Gross margin expandingIs it keeping more of each sale?
9Asset turnover improvingIs it generating more sales per unit of assets?

Altman Z''-score — distance from financial distress

Edward Altman (1968) fitted a formula on companies that later went bankrupt versus ones that survived. It combines working capital, retained earnings, operating profit and equity — each scaled by total assets — into a single distance-from-distress number. QuantDesk uses the Z'' emerging-market variant so an Indonesian listing and a US one land on the same scale.

  • Above 2.6 — comfortably outside the distress zone.
  • 1.1 to 2.6 — the grey zone. Neither safe nor distressed.
  • Below 1.1 — inside the distress zone.

Working capital, if it's new to you, is current assets minus current liabilities — roughly, what the company could pay its near-term bills with.

Beneish M-Score — does this look like manipulated earnings?

Messod Beneish (1999) built a model from eight ratios comparing this year to last, fitted on companies later found to have manipulated their earnings. Above −1.78 is a flag; below −2.22 is clean; between them is borderline. The eight inputs:

IndexFull nameWhat a high reading suggests
DSRIDays sales in receivablesSales booked but not yet collected — possibly aggressive revenue recognition
GMIGross marginMargins deteriorating, creating pressure to flatter results
AQIAsset qualityMore of the balance sheet in soft, hard-to-verify assets
SGISales growthRapid growth — not fraud itself, but the environment where it happens
DEPIDepreciationSlowing depreciation, which flatters current profit
SGAISelling, general & adminOverheads rising faster than sales
LVGILeverageDebt rising relative to assets
TATATotal accruals to assetsProfit increasingly made of accounting entries rather than cash

Accruals is the key word. Accounting profit records revenue when earned and costs when incurred, not when cash moves; the gap between profit and cash is accruals. It is where judgement lives, which is where manipulation lives.

How to read a Beneish flag — this matters

Beneish catches roughly three-quarters of actual manipulators. That sounds strong until you apply it to a population where manipulation is rare. If 1 in 100 companies manipulates, a screen that catches 75% of them while false-alarming on 5% of honest ones produces about 0.75 true flags and 5 false ones per hundred companies — so most flags are false alarms. This is the base-rate problem, and it is one of the most important ideas in all of applied statistics. A flag means “look closer”, never “fraud found.”

Why banks get a refusal instead of a score

None of the three models was built on financial firms, and none transfers. A bank has no operating cycle for “working capital” to describe; its revenue is not a receivables-and-inventory process. So for a bank or insurer, QuantDesk prints an explicit refusal rather than a number. A score there would be arithmetic without meaning — and printing it anyway is how tools lose the right to be trusted on the numbers they can compute.

08 — Breadth

Scan & rank: from a universe to a shortlist

Everything above examines one company. Professionals work in two tiers: a script ranks a large universe on cheap signals, then a human examines the top few in depth. QuantDesk's Scan & rank tab is the first tier.

  • Universe. The list of stocks you're comparing — the Dow 30, the Nasdaq-100, Indonesia's LQ45 or IDX30, or your own pasted list.
  • Cross-sectional. Comparing many stocks at one moment, as opposed to one stock across time. The whole ranking tier is cross-sectional.

Every signal is converted to a percentile within the scan before anything is combined, and each signal's weight is set by how well the effect is supported, not how interesting it is.

Note that three of those seven — momentum, near-its-high, and versus-the-index — are three ways of saying “it went up.” A composite that averages them looks like a consensus of three tests when it is closer to one.

Effective signals: the best idea in the app
What it does
Rather than warn you about that redundancy in a footnote, the panel measures it. It computes the rank correlation between every pair of signals across the actual scan, then reports the participation ratio of that correlation matrix — a number that equals the column count when signals are independent and collapses toward 1 as they become redundant.
A real result
On a Dow scan, momentum and trend correlated at +0.98, and the seven columns carried about 3.4 signals' worth of independent information. That number is printed in the header.
Why it is worth copying
It converts a caveat into a measurement the reader can check. Anyone can write “these signals overlap”; showing that seven is really 3.4 is a different kind of claim.

Why quality and valuation are a second step

Daily price history batches — one request fetches fifty symbols, so ranking a hundred names costs a handful of calls. Financial statements do not; they come one company at a time and take seconds each. That is a hard architectural constraint, not a design preference, and it is why the ranking table has no Quality column. You pick a shortlist of up to eight and deepen those. Showing an empty Quality column for two hundred names would be worse than admitting the limit.

Index lists go stale, invisibly

Index membership is transcribed by hand and stamped with the date it was written. It decays silently: a dropped constituent still fetches happily, and a newly added one is simply absent. That is why the date is shown on the panel. There is deliberately no S&P 500 list — five hundred symbols is the length at which transcription goes wrong, and a mistyped ticker produces a plausible ranking row for a company nobody asked about rather than an error.

The same machinery, pointed at one company

Everything above ranks a universe so you can pick a few names from it. The identical scan also answers a question for a company you have already chosen: where does it sit among its own peers? That is the Compare with peers panel on the Trend tab, covered in section 05 — same percentiles, same measured overlap, one row instead of a table.

The anomaly screener, which answers a different question

Ranking asks “which of these is best on standing characteristics?” The screener asks “has something unusual just happened here?” It scans up to 20 tickers and — crucially — reports how many hits you should have expected from pure chance. That leads directly into the next section.

09 — The part that makes you a quant

The honesty layer: six ideas that separate analysis from astrology

Everything up to here was what the app computes. This section is why you should or shouldn't believe it, and it is the genuinely transferable material. These six ideas apply to every model you will ever build, in any field.

1 · Multiple testing — hits appear by construction

Scan twenty stocks for “unusual activity” at a 5% threshold and you will average one hit even if nothing unusual is happening anywhere. Scan two hundred and you get ten. The hits are not evidence; they are arithmetic.

QuantDesk's screener therefore tests each ticker against that ticker's own long-run flag rate, then applies a Benjamini–Hochberg correction across the whole scan. The result is a q-value per row — the probability that this particular hit is a false alarm given that you went looking across many names — and a headline saying how many hits noise alone would have produced.

  • p-value. The probability of seeing a result at least this extreme if nothing real were going on. Small p, surprising result.
  • False discovery rate (FDR). Of the things you flagged, what share are expected to be wrong. More useful than trying to make each individual test perfect.

2 · The event study — does the signal predict anything?

Almost every anomaly-detection tool sold to retail investors asserts that its signal works. QuantDesk ships a button that measures it. The event study takes every anomaly the Flow lens ever detected on a ticker, measures the cumulative abnormal return afterwards, and reports a t-statistic.

  • Abnormal return. The return that isn't explained by the market moving. If the index rose 2% and a high-beta stock rose 2%, its abnormal return is negative — it underperformed what its market sensitivity predicted.
  • Market model. The regression used to work out what a stock “should have” done given the index. Abnormal return is the leftover.
  • t-statistic. How many standard errors an estimate sits from zero. Roughly: above about 2, the effect is unlikely to be chance alone. Below, you have not shown anything.
The result that proves the tool is honest

Run on JPMorgan, the event study returned no significant effect at any horizon — and the app reports that plainly. A tool that only ever confirms itself is worth nothing. When you build your own signals, the moment you start explaining away null results is the moment you stop doing research.

3 · In-sample versus out-of-sample

If you tune a rule on a stretch of history and then test it on that same stretch, you have measured how well you tuned, not how well the rule works. That is in-sample testing, and it is the most common way financial research fools its own author.

Out-of-sample testing evaluates on data the model never saw. Walk-forward is the disciplined version for time series: fit on data up to time T, predict T+1, roll forward, repeat — so no future information ever leaks backwards. QuantDesk's anomaly detector has a walk-forward mode, and the app is candid that it is off by default because it costs minutes per ticker.

Look-ahead bias is the general name for the sin: using information that would not have been available at the time. It is astonishingly easy to commit by accident — a moving average computed over the whole series, a universe of “today's index members” applied to ten years ago (that second one is survivorship bias, and it flatters every backtest that commits it).

4 · Estimator resolution — knowing what your instrument can see

Covered in the Flow section, but it generalises. Two numbers in this app are reported as bounds rather than measurements, because that is what the data supports: the bid–ask spread cannot be resolved below roughly 0.15× a stock's daily volatility from daily bars, and the Hurst exponent's random-walk band has to be sized from the sample. Both floors were measured by simulation, not assumed.

The habit to steal: when you build an estimator, feed it data where you already know the true answer — including the case where the true answer is zero — and find out what it reports. That tells you your resolution. Anything finer than it is noise wearing a number's clothes.

5 · Correlated signals aren't independent evidence

Four panels reading two datasets are not four opinions. Seven ranking columns carrying 3.4 signals' worth of information are not seven tests. Averaging correlated things and presenting the result as a consensus overstates your evidence — and it is the single easiest way to build a model that looks robust and isn't.

Note the distinction the app draws between its treatments of this. The ranking panel measures its overlap, because a scan across many names gives it a cross-section to measure from. The confluence rail declares its grouping — which lens reads which data is a statement about the code, not something you could measure. Knowing which of those two situations you are in is the skill.

But the consequence of a declared grouping can be measured, and now is. Run all four lenses over 168 companies and you can ask how often the price family and the filings family actually reach the same verdict — against how often their own habits would put them there anyway. That second number is the one people skip. Two lenses that each say “good” about 70% of the time agree 58% of the time knowing nothing about each other, and 58% reported on its own reads like strong corroboration. Subtract it and the two families come out at κ = +0.03: they agree about as often as chance, which is what the rail needed to be true.

The same run turned up something the app had assumed and never checked. Flow and Trend are collapsed into one vote because they read the same price series — and their verdicts agree at κ = +0.03 too, which is to say hardly at all. Reading the same data and reaching unrelated conclusions is perfectly possible; they pull different things out of it. So why not count them separately and claim more independence? Because a reading that correlates with nothing looks exactly like a reading that means nothing. Both are uncorrelated with everything else. The Flow lens's own event study (section 09) usually finds no effect, so the honest move was to keep the conservative count and say why — which is the whole habit this manual is trying to teach.

And the synthesis (section 03) takes the third option: rather than combine the four readings at all, it prints them, says which of them rest on the same data, and names the places they conflict. Refusing to aggregate is a legitimate answer, and it is usually the honest one when you cannot measure how much your inputs overlap.

6 · Reported figures are a chain of choices

Every number you see is the end of a chain: which data source, which date range, which estimator, which threshold. Change any link and the number changes. This is why QuantDesk exposes the price's own provenance (“close of 2026-08-21”), why it names which engine produced a valuation, and why every assumption in the DCF is an editable field.

When someone shows you a financial number without its chain, the missing chain is the finding.

Known limits, stated by the app itself

  • The data source. Prices and filings come from a free, unofficial scraper of an undocumented endpoint, with no service guarantee. Fundamentals for smaller Indonesian listings are patchy. This is the single biggest fragility in the project, and the app says so.
  • Results are in-sample, on one ticker at a time, with overlapping windows. The event study is indicative, not a backtest.
  • Deliberately not implemented: order-flow toxicity measures (they need trade-level data a daily approximation cannot stand in for), multi-bar chart patterns, and calendar effects — where the multiple-testing critique bites hardest of all.

11 — The honest path

How you actually become a quant

Reading this manual will let you use QuantDesk properly and hold your own in a conversation about market data. It will not make you a quant, and it would be a disservice to imply otherwise. Here is what the gap actually consists of.

The four things the job is made of

  1. Statistics, deeply. Not formulas — inference. Sampling distributions, standard errors, hypothesis testing and its abuses, regression and what violating its assumptions does to you, multiple comparisons, Bayesian reasoning about base rates. Section 09 is a tour of this; the real thing is a couple of years.
  2. Programming, fluently. Python with numpy and pandas is the working language of this field, and QuantDesk is written in exactly that stack. Being able to read api/_lib/ and reproduce one of its estimators from the paper is a genuinely good milestone.
  3. Mathematics. Linear algebra and probability first; calculus and optimisation after. Stochastic calculus only if you go toward derivatives pricing — most quant work does not need it, whatever the reading lists say.
  4. Market structure. How orders actually match, what a market maker does, how fees and spreads and borrowing costs eat returns. This is the part self-taught quants skip and professionals consider table stakes, because it is where paper strategies go to die.

A sequence that works

  1. Use this app on ten companies you know, reading every explanation. You are building vocabulary and, more importantly, calibration.
  2. Reproduce one number by hand. Pick something small — maximum drawdown, or RSI. Pull the price history, compute it yourself in Python, and match the app. You will learn more from the mismatch than the match.
  3. Read the code for an estimator you now understand. The docstrings in this project explain why each choice was made and what failed before it. That is rare and it is the best free teaching material you have to hand.
  4. Break something on purpose. Feed a model garbage — a flat price series, two data points, a stock that halved overnight — and see whether it refuses or invents. Learning where models fail is the actual skill.
  5. Form a hypothesis and test it out-of-sample. Something narrow and falsifiable. Then accept the answer, including when it's no. Especially when it's no.
  6. Learn where the data is wrong. Survivorship bias, corporate actions, restated filings, delisted tickers. Every serious practitioner spends more time on data quality than on models, and nobody warns you.
The disposition that matters more than the curriculum

Markets are close to random over short horizons, competitive over long ones, and generous to nobody. The people who last are not the ones with the cleverest model — they are the ones who know exactly how much their model does not know, and size their conviction accordingly. Every design decision in this app points the same direction: report the uncertainty, refuse when the model doesn't apply, and publish the null result.

That is the whole job, and you can start practising it today with no mathematics at all.

Not investment advice. QuantDesk is educational and research tooling. Prices and filings come from Yahoo Finance — unaudited, occasionally incomplete, and never a substitute for reading the filings yourself. Every model described here is an estimate built on assumptions you can and should change.
